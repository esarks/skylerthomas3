## The Woman at the Well

There's an ancient story that captures this threshold moment perfectly.

It's noon—the hottest part of the day. No one comes to the well at noon if they can help it. You come in the morning or evening when it's cooler.

But this woman comes at noon. Alone. Because she can't face the other women. Can't bear the stares, the whispers, the judgment. Five marriages. Five. And the man she's with now isn't even her husband.

So she comes when no one else is there.

Except today, there's a Jewish man sitting by the well. And He speaks to her.

Jewish men don't speak to Samaritan women. Ever. It's a cultural line you don't cross.

But Jesus crosses it. He asks her for a drink.

The conversation starts surface-level:

**Woman:** "You're a Jew. I'm a Samaritan. Why are you even talking to me?"

But Jesus goes deeper:

**Jesus:** "If you knew the gift of God and who it is that asks you for a drink, you would have asked him and he would have given you living water."

**Woman:** "You have nothing to draw with and the well is deep. Where can you get this living water?"

Still surface. Practical. Safe. But Jesus goes deeper:

**Jesus:** "Everyone who drinks this water will be thirsty again, but whoever drinks the water I give them will never thirst. Indeed, the water I give them will become in them a spring of water welling up to eternal life."

**Woman:** "Sir, give me this water so that I won't get thirsty and have to keep coming here to draw water!"

Getting closer. She's interested. But she's still thinking about physical water. Jesus goes even deeper:

**Jesus:** "Go, call your husband and come back."

There it is. The question she dreads.

**Woman:** "I have no husband."

**Jesus:** "You are right when you say you have no husband. The fact is, you have had five husbands, and the man you now have is not your husband. What you have just said is quite true."

Now we're at the wound. The shame. The thing she came at noon to avoid facing.

He sees her. Completely. Knows her history. Knows her shame. Knows her secrets.

And He doesn't condemn. Doesn't lecture. Doesn't pull back in disgust.

He just... sees her. And offers her living water anyway.

*What would it feel like to be truly seen—completely known—and not condemned?*

This is the scandal of the threshold: You don't have to clean up before you step in. You come as you are—covered in swamp, carrying your failures, dragging your history—and the water doesn't recoil.

From this depth—the place of wound and shame and honesty—she encounters Jesus not as a prophet, but as the Messiah. And everything changes.

She leaves her water jar behind. No longer hiding. No longer coming at noon to avoid people.

She runs back to town and tells everyone: "Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Could this be the Messiah?"

The woman who came in shame leaves as an evangelist. The woman who came thirsty for physical water leaves having drunk from the source of living water.

That's what happens at the water's edge. Transformation doesn't flow from surface encounters. It flows from depth—from the place where we let ourselves be fully known and discover we're fully loved anyway.
